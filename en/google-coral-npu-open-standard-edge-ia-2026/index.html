Google Opened Its Edge AI Chip: The AI That Doesn't Send Your Data to the Cloud | inicIA

By inicIA · October 21, 2026 · 6 min read

Direct answer

On October 20, 2026, Google Research announced it had completed the 32-bit Coral NPU architecture and published the full design (RTL) on GitHub under the Apache 2.0 license, alongside version 1.0 of the open-source compiler (IREE/MLIR) and the MPACT simulator. The same day, at Synaptics Tech Day, Synaptics launched commercial products built on that technology, including the Astra SR200 series (400 GOPS in an ultra-low-power package for wearables and industrial sensor nodes) and the Coralboard SR developer kit. The stated goal: solving edge-AI hardware fragmentation —hundreds of proprietary, incompatible architectures— with an open, RISC-V-based standard design and a single toolchain, so a team optimizes a model once and deploys it across multiple chips. On the commercial side, Synaptics' Astra SL2610/SL2600 microcontroller already exists, combining Google's Coral NPU "M2" with Synaptics' Torq NPU and Arm CPUs, with on-device inference at an average of under 6 milliwatts (Google's figure; the executive presenting it did not specify the workloads or comparison processors). The Google–Synaptics partnership dates to January 2025; the chip was previewed at Google's Research@ event on October 14. Google is also working with VeriSilicon (design verification) and GlobalFoundries (12LP+ FinFET process). (Sources: Google Developers Blog, cryptobriefing, R&D World, alfw.ai.)

Technical honesty: what it is and what it is not

  • It is: a real, open hardware design, with public compiler and simulator —anyone can inspect it. A serious step toward an open edge-AI ecosystem.
  • It is not (yet): a formally ratified industry standard; the power figures are targets; and seamless portability of the same compiled model across different Coral chips is not yet demonstrated with public documentation (alfw.ai analysis).
  • The "40% less energy" Google cites is launch marketing: without published workloads or comparables, take it as direction, not benchmark.

Why it matters to your business

  1. Privacy by architecture: on-device AI does not send your data to the cloud. For clinics, law firms, finance and any business with sensitive or regulated data, edge AI removes an entire class of risks (and contract clauses).
  2. Latency and autonomy: wearables, industrial sensors, retail and logistics need millisecond responses without depending on the network. That is where edge applications live.
  3. Operating cost: milliwatt local inference means battery-lasting devices with no API bill per query. The cost model changes.
  4. Ecosystem bet: an open standard reduces the risk of being locked into proprietary silicon. If you will invest in AI hardware, ask about open standards before signing onto closed architectures.
  5. Not for your office tomorrow: this is 2026–2028 infrastructure. The actionable move today is to design your AI projects separating what belongs in the cloud from what could move to the edge as hardware matures.

Frequently asked questions

What is Coral NPU?

Google's neural processing unit for edge AI: hardware designed to run AI models directly on devices (wearables, sensors, robotics) at very low power. Google published its 32-bit design as an open standard on October 20, 2026.

What did Synaptics announce?

At its October 20, 2026 Tech Day it launched three products on Coral NPU technology, including the Astra SR200 series (400 GOPS, ultra-low-power) and the Coralboard SR developer kit, with the open IREE/MLIR toolchain.

What does RISC-V mean?

An open instruction-set architecture (no proprietary licenses) on which Coral NPU is built. Being open reduces licensing costs and dependence on a single chip vendor.

When does this affect my company?

Commercial hardware already exists (Astra SL2610) and the ecosystem is forming; mass product adoption will arrive over the next 1–2 years. Today, the practical decision is architectural: separating cloud and edge workloads in your projects.
