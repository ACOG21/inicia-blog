Canada bought a piece of Cohere for up to $1.6B: sovereign AI is paid with capital, not speeches

By inicIA · November 1, 2026 · 6 min read

Direct answer

Prime Minister Mark Carney announced on October 29, 2026 in Montreal that the Government of Canada will make an equity investment of up to C$1.6 billion, about US$1.1 billion, in Cohere, the Toronto-founded AI company building secure systems for businesses and governments. The investment is commercial and staged, alongside private investors including NVIDIA, Bell and Schwarz Group, and flows through the Canada Development Investment Corporation. The caveat that defines the announcement: Canada buys an equity stake, not a grant, and the figure is a committed ceiling, not a payment already completed.

The numbers and their labels

  • The round: Cohere announced the initial close of a Series E seeking up to C$7 billion, at a C$35.7 billion post-money valuation, per BetaKit. The full round is a target, not money already raised.
  • Cohere's commitments: keep its incorporation and headquarters in Canada, create at least 1,000 high-paying Canadian technical jobs by 2030 and commit at least C$1.7 billion in additional Canadian computing infrastructure by 2030, with regular progress reporting.
  • The company's broader projection: Cohere said on October 30 that its Canada investment effort, begun in 2024, now projects more than 1,500 Canadian jobs and more than C$4 billion in research and development and AI infrastructure through 2031. That is a company projection, not a commitment in the government announcement.
  • The business behind the bet: Cohere reports 165% year-over-year annual recurring revenue growth as of the third quarter of 2026 and about 300 customers. The company is private and has not published a 2026 revenue figure.
  • The April 2026 precedent: Cohere agreed to merge with Germany's Aleph Alpha, with Cohere shareholders keeping about 90% and the company staying headquartered in Canada.

The reading for your business

  • Sovereignty is bought with conditions: the Canadian model ties public capital to headquarters, jobs and compute inside the country, with staged disbursements subject to reporting. A government that pays in stages can verify; a government that pays at once can only hope.
  • Mid-size providers gain a new backstop: for a business buying enterprise AI, a provider with a state as shareholder and government customers has a different stability profile than a lab dependent only on private rounds. That stability does not guarantee a better product, but it changes counterparty risk.
  • The open question: the announcement does not specify the government's final ownership percentage. The real size of the stake will emerge with the staged closings and the committed reporting.
