An AI Won an Art Contest in 2022: The IP Lesson for Your Business | inicIA

By inicIA · October 9, 2026 · 7 min read

Direct answer

On August 29, 2022, Jason M. Allen won the Colorado State Fair's annual fine art competition with "Théâtre D'opéra Spatial," a piece created with the Midjourney image generator: one of the first AI-generated images to win such a prize, with a $300 cash award. Allen disclosed from the start that he had used Midjourney, worked through more than 624 prompts and revisions over roughly 80 hours, and retouched the image in Photoshop. The judges later said they did not know Midjourney used AI, but would have awarded him first place anyway. The lesson for your business is not the scandal: it is the still-open question —who owns the content your company generates with AI?— and why you must document the human contribution in every piece.

What exactly happened

Allen, a video game designer from Pueblo, Colorado, entered the "Digital Arts/Digitally-Manipulated Photography" category of the emerging-artist division (18 images by 11 participants). He submitted three pieces credited "Jason Allen via Midjourney" and printed them on canvas for display. When he posted his win on social media, the piece went viral for the wrong reasons: artists accused him of cheating and of hastening "the death of art." Allen replied that he had set out to make a statement about AI art and would not apologize. Two of his pieces later sold for $750 each.

The point that matters for your company

The "is it art?" debate is over. What remains live is the legal and commercial problem: in the United States, the Copyright Office has held that purely AI-generated works, without sufficient human authorship, are not registrable. Translated to your business:

  1. What your AI generates is not automatically "yours" in the strong IP sense. If your logo, catalog, or campaign images come out of a generator, your legal protection is weaker than you think.
  2. Documented human contribution is your defense. The prompts, the iterations, the Photoshop editing, the curation: keep the process record. The more demonstrable human input, the stronger your position.
  3. Read your tool's terms. Who may use the image, whether the vendor keeps licenses, whether third parties can generate something identical: it is in the terms you accepted without reading.

The 3 rules for AI content in your business

  1. Document the process. Save prompts, versions, and edits for every important piece. It is your evidence of human authorship.
  2. Do not build your brand on a single generated image. If that image is your core asset and its protection is weak, your brand rests on sand.
  3. Separate the fungible from the strategic. Use AI freely for fungible content (posts, variants, drafts). For strategic assets (logo, identity, flagship pieces), combine AI with documented human creation and legal counsel.

Frequently asked questions

Did Allen cheat?

Not under the rules: the guidelines defined digital art as practice using digital technology in the creative process, and he disclosed Midjourney use when submitting. The judges said they would have awarded him first place anyway.

Can I copyright an AI-generated image?

In the US, the Copyright Office's position is that sufficient human authorship is required. An image from a simple prompt is unlikely to qualify; documented human contribution (editing, composition, curation) strengthens the case. Consult an attorney for your specific situation.

Does this affect my AI-generated product photos?

Yes: if your product images are 100% generated, a competitor could generate something similar and your ability to stop them is limited. For catalogs, real photos remain the most protectable asset.

Do 624 prompts count as "authorship"?

That is the argument in your favor: they show sustained human creative direction, not one click. Hence the rule: document the full process, not just the result.
