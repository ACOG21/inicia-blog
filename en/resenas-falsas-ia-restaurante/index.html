Singapore Caught 100 Businesses Buying AI-Written Fake Reviews: 45 Must Apologize for 6 Months | inicIA

By inicIA · October 11, 2026 · 6 min read

Direct answer

On September 30, 2026, Singapore's Competition and Consumer Commission (CCS) announced its largest fake-review investigation to date: about 100 businesses — from medical clinics to law firms, restaurants, hair salons, and funeral services — bought fabricated reviews from a provider that used generative AI to make them look real. In the first phase, 45 businesses admitted buying the reviews and accepted the remedies: stop the practice, remove all fabricated ratings, and publish a public apology for six continuous months — not just on their websites and social media, but on physical notices at their premises. Among the first to start apologizing: Japanese restaurant Torio, Harmony Funeral Care, and Picasso Hair Boutique. The lesson: AI made fake reviews cheaper, and it also made detecting them cheaper.

How the operation worked

The provider was Reputifly, run by its sole director Julian Tung Yan Kai, selling reviews through two sites: BuyReviewSG and GetReviewSG. The service had operated since 2023 or earlier. Businesses bought packages — for example, 35 five-star reviews over seven days for S$219 — and there was even a calculator to work out how many five-star reviews they needed to reach their desired Google rating. The reviews appeared on Google, Facebook, Tripadvisor, Carousell, Yelp, and Trustpilot.

Generative AI was the heart of the deception: it varied writing styles and sentence structures, incorporated Singapore-context details, and introduced natural imperfections. Clients could edit the generated reviews before publication. Over time, the system grew more sophisticated: an online dashboard generated, managed, and tracked the fake reviews, and could train on the business's own real four- and five-star Google reviews to produce new ones with relevant details. Some fake reviews deliberately described initial reservations before recounting a positive experience, creating an illusion of authenticity.

The punishment and the message

Reputifly admitted to aiding and abetting unfair trade practices, agreed to stop providing the service, and — in an unusual requirement — must donate its proceeds to a registered charity, without claiming tax deductions on the donation. The 45 cooperating businesses gave formal undertakings to the commission; the two that declined to cooperate remain under investigation in a second phase. The CCS invited other providers and businesses to come forward voluntarily before it opens formal investigations: early cooperation will be viewed favorably. The regulator's message was direct: fake reviews deceive consumers, distort competition, and disadvantage businesses that compete honestly.

The lesson for your business

AI did two things at once: it made fake reviews cheaper and more convincing, and it made their detection more likely. Three rules that remain:

  1. Never buy reviews. Saving S$219 per package turned into six months of public apology at the store's front door. The math does not work.
  2. Real reviews are earned with systems, not tricks. A post-purchase flow inviting feedback, public replies to every review (including the bad ones), and service worth five stars: that is what no regulator can punish.
  3. Watch your profile. If a competitor or vendor "gifts" you fake reviews without your consent, detecting it late exposes you too. Review your profiles periodically.
