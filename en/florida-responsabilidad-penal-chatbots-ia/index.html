Florida Wants Criminal Liability for Chatbots That Help Commit Crimes: How to Shield Your Agents | inicIA

By inicIA · October 8, 2026 · 7 min read

Direct answer

On September 8, 2026, Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier proposed applying the state's aider-and-abettor criminal law to companies that own, control, or distribute AI chatbots that participate in criminal activity. The target: companies with "practical control" over a system's design, training, deployment, or safety settings. Possible sanctions after a conviction include heavy fines, payments to victims, court-ordered monitoring of the business, and even suspension of operations in Florida. The proposal is still under development and has not been introduced or approved by the legislature. But the message for your business is clear: the agents you deploy are no longer "just software" in the eyes of the law.

Florida's escalation, in three acts

This did not come out of nowhere. Florida has spent months building a deliberate strategy:

  1. April 2026: criminal investigation into AI's role in the Florida State University shooting (investigators say the accused asked ChatGPT about firearms, ammunition, shooting distances, and times with more people on campus; he has not been convicted).
  2. June 1, 2026: civil lawsuit against OpenAI and Sam Altman for deceptive practices — the first state-led action of its kind.
  3. September 8, 2026: the criminal-liability proposal unveiled in Tampa.
  4. September 28, 2026: motion for a temporary injunction against OpenAI in the Tenth Judicial Circuit, asking the court to halt new model development without independent safety guardrails.

Cases cited by the AG's office include the FSU case chats, questions about disposing of a body in another case, AI-generated sexual images of minors, scams, voice cloning, and unsafe medical advice.

What the proposal actually does

It creates no new agency and no technical checklist. The idea is more direct: if the product takes part in the crime, the company that designed it, trained it, and collects the subscription answers as an accomplice. Prosecutors would examine a company's real control over four things: system design, training, deployment, and safety settings for Florida users. And per the AG, the proposal regulates criminal conduct involving AI without banning the technology.

The 4 shields for your business agents

Regardless of whether the law passes, these four shields protect you today and prepare you for tomorrow:

  1. Written, tested guardrails. Define prohibited topics for your agents (instructions for wrongdoing, sexual content involving minors, definitive medical or legal advice) and actively test them: try to break them yourself every quarter.
  2. Documented red-teaming. Keep evidence that you probed your agents' limits and fixed what you found. In an investigation, the record of diligent testing is your best defense.
  3. Logs and traceability. Retain records of your agents' relevant interactions (with a clear retention policy and privacy notice). Without logs, you cannot prove your agent behaved.
  4. Human escalation. Every customer-facing agent needs a path to a human and autonomy limits: what it may resolve alone, and what it must always escalate.

Frequently asked questions

Is the law in force already?

No. It is a proposal by the attorney general unveiled September 8, 2026; it remains under development and has not been introduced or approved by Florida lawmakers.

Does it affect my business if I use a big vendor's chatbot?

The proposal targets companies with "practical control" over design, training, deployment, or safety. If you only use a third party's tool, the main target is the vendor; but as a deployer you must still configure the tool properly and never use it for illicit purposes.

What does "practical control" mean?

That the company decides how the system is designed, trained, deployed, and how its safety is configured. Whoever holds those keys carries the responsibility.

Should I shut down my agents as a precaution?

No. The proposal does not ban the technology. The prudent move is to document your guardrails, test your agents, and have human escalation: operate with diligence, not fear.
