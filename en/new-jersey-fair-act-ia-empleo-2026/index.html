New Jersey Advances the FAIR Act: Your Hiring and Employee-Monitoring AI Under New Rules | inicIA

By inicIA · October 18, 2026 · 6 min read

Direct answer

On October 1, 2026, the New Jersey Senate Labor Committee voted 5-0 for the Fair AI Regulations (FAIR) Act (S4075) — sponsored by Senator Andrew Zwicker — and referred it to the Senate Budget and Appropriations Committee. Mind the status: it is a bill, not an enacted law. If passed in its current form, it would regulate three technology categories: (1) electronic monitoring tools (EMT) that collect data on workers and applicants; (2) automated employment decision systems (AEDS) used in hiring, firing, discipline, scheduling, and evaluations; and (3) automated public-benefit and service decision systems. It would take effect 18 months after enactment.

What it regulates, in facts

  • Electronic monitoring (EMT): tools that collect and process data about workers and applicants — keystrokes, mouse movements, idle time, communications — by means other than direct observation by a person.
  • Automated employment decisions (AEDS): any computational process (machine learning, statistical modeling, analytics, AI) whose output an employer uses in decisions affecting "terms and conditions of employment": wages, schedules, performance evaluations, discipline, promotion, and assignments.
  • Public benefits: systems deciding who receives government benefits and services.
  • AI does not decide alone: the bill bars surveillance data or an automated output from being the sole or determinative basis of a hiring, firing, or discipline decision. It requires real human review — with training, resources, and authority to throw out biased data — not a rubber-stamp "accept" click.
  • Transparency and worker voice: advance written notice before deploying these tools (union analysis of the bill cites 60 days), disclosure of performance standards, access to the data, and a real opportunity to challenge errors.
  • Anti-retaliation: employees are protected if they refuse to follow an AI system's output they reasonably believe will cause bodily injury or property damage.

Why it matters even if you are not in New Jersey

New Jersey joins a wave that is already national: Connecticut's law took effect October 1, the EU fines under the AI Act, and Washington debates mandatory rules. The pattern is identical everywhere — advance transparency, human review with real authority, and a ban on 100% automated decisions. If your company operates across states, design your processes to the highest standard and you will not have to rebuild them every time a new state legislates.

What to do starting today

  1. Map your monitoring: inventory everything collecting employee data — productivity software, cameras with analytics, keyloggers, call recording — and which decisions it feeds.
  2. Audit your automated decisions: does any HR tool decide or "recommend" firings, bonuses, or promotions without documented human review? That is the first thing the law will look at.
  3. Draft the notice now: write the disclosure today — which tool, what data it collects, for which decision; when the law arrives, you just publish it.
  4. Train your human reviewers: the review the law demands is not paperwork; whoever reviews must understand the tool and have the authority to say no.

Frequently asked questions

Is the FAIR Act already law?

No. On October 1, 2026 the NJ Senate Labor Committee approved it 5-0 and referred it to the Budget and Appropriations Committee. It still needs floor votes and the governor's signature.

What three things does it regulate?

Electronic monitoring tools for workers (EMT), automated employment decision systems like hiring and firing (AEDS), and automated public-benefit and service decision systems.

When would it take effect?

18 months after becoming law, in its current draft.

Can AI fire someone on its own under the FAIR Act?

No: the bill bars surveillance data or an automated output from being the sole or determinative basis of employment decisions, and requires human review with real authority to discard biased or flawed data.
