Altman Lays Out Three Paths for AI Safety as Labs Build a Common Standard for 2027

By inicIA · October 25, 2026 · 6 min read

Direct answer

According to The Information on October 23, 2026, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman described three possible approaches to AI safety, at a time when the biggest labs are debating how to test their models and who should oversee them. In parallel, OpenAI, Google and Anthropic are working on a US-based group called the Standards Authority for Frontier AI, which they aim to launch in early 2027. Anthropic also proposed draft legislation that would let the US Commerce Department halt models capable of causing large-scale harm. AI safety is moving from a lab debate to market infrastructure, and it will reach your contracts.

The context: why the debate is accelerating

  • Recent incidents at every lab: the discussion follows security incidents involving models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Meta, plus greater competitive pressure as public listings approach.
  • A voluntary accord with no teeth: on September 29, executives from Anthropic, Google, Meta, OpenAI, Nvidia and xAI signed a voluntary accord that President Trump described as "morally binding", but which creates no legal liability.
  • Voluntary pre-access: under the current voluntary framework, developers give access to certain models up to 30 days before release. The debate now is whether that access should be mandatory, what gets tested and who verifies.
  • Differences among labs: Altman and Anthropic differ on how new models should be tested and how much regulatory oversight is needed. Some OpenAI employees read Altman's framing as a message aimed at Anthropic, given one of its critics' ties to that company.

The three paths, in business terms

  • Path 1, shared industry standards: labs agree on how to test and what to publish. Advantage: speed and technical knowledge. Risk: the standard gets written by the same companies that must comply with it.
  • Path 2, public oversight with power to stop: a regulator can halt a dangerous model. Advantage: real teeth. Risk: slowness and politicization of technical decisions.
  • Path 3, verifiable third-party transparency: independent evaluators with real access. Advantage: trust without slowing innovation. Risk: it depends on access being genuine, not a showcase.
  • The date that matters: the Standards Authority for Frontier AI targets early 2027. If your business buys AI today, the standards governing your providers a year from now are being written right now.

What to do in your business starting today

  1. Ask your provider for its safety report: if a provider cannot show how it tests its models or what incidents it has had, that silence is information.
  2. Demand independent evaluators in large contracts: a provider's self-assessment is a starting point, not a guarantee.
  3. Document your own decisions: record which model you use, for what, with which data and with what human oversight. When regulation arrives, that record is your best defence.
  4. Separate the tool from the risk: the question is what it can do without human oversight in your business. That is where the real risk lives.

The honest note

  • The exact content of the three approaches comes from a report by The Information: Altman described the paths in internal discussions reported by that outlet. It is not a public OpenAI document, and details may change.
  • The Standards Authority does not exist yet: it is an ongoing effort with an early-2027 target date, not an operating entity with published rules.
  • Sources: The Information via Storyboard18 (October 23, 2026) and the context of the voluntary accord of September 29, 2026.

Frequently asked questions

What did Sam Altman propose on AI safety?

According to The Information, he described three possible approaches to AI safety, amid the debate over how to test new models and who should oversee them.

What is the Standards Authority for Frontier AI?

A US-based group being built by OpenAI, Google and Anthropic, with an early-2027 launch target, to set common standards for frontier models.

Is there mandatory federal AI safety regulation in the US today?

No, at the general federal level. The current framework is voluntary, including the September 29, 2026 accord, which creates no legal liability.

What should my business do in the meantime?

Ask providers for safety reports, document which model you use for what and with what human oversight, and separate the tool from the risk it can create unsupervised.
