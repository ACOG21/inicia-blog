Apollo: AI still does not pay outside the Magnificent 7 — the number your budget should read calmly

By inicIA · October 26, 2026 · 6 min read

Direct answer

In a note published on Saturday, October 24, 2026, Torsten Sløk, chief economist at Apollo Global Management, wrote that, despite massive investment and attention on artificial intelligence, the technology has not yet delivered a financial payoff to corporate America outside the tech sector. His comparison is index-level: revenue per worker rises for the Mag 7 (Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia and Tesla) and is flat for the S&P 493 (the S&P 500 minus those seven); the same holds for profit margins. And he adds: there are still no signs that AI is boosting productivity growth in that group. Bloomberg carried the note with the same wording, and an independent check verified the quotes against Apollo's original document.

What it says exactly, and what it does not say

  • It is an index comparison, not a company-by-company verdict: the S&P 493 groups 493 companies. A flat aggregate does not mean no company outside the Mag 7 gains from AI.
  • The second circulating quote is also verbatim: according to Sløk, mainstream corporate America is pulling back and starting to view token spending as a cost center weighing on margins, not a source of growth.
  • The author leaves the question open: Sløk asks whether the S&P 493's flat margins will persist, rather than answering it. A headline presenting it as a final verdict goes further than the note.
  • The investment context does not vanish: AI spending nears $400 billion in 2026 per IDC, and only 23% of companies scale agents per McKinsey, figures this blog covered on October 16. Apollo's note describes aggregate payoff so far, not the impossibility of earning it.

How to use this number in your budget

  1. Measure your own payoff, not the index's: define before buying which task improves, by how much, over what time, and at what cost per unit. Without that baseline, your project is indistinguishable from the spending Sløk describes.
  2. Separate token spending from process investment: paying for more tokens without changing the process that uses them is the cost center in the note. Changing the process is the investment.
  3. Demand a use case with an owner and a date: an agent with no internal owner and no review date is an eternal pilot, and the eternal pilot is exactly what the aggregate index is measuring.
  4. Compare cost per outcome, not cost per token: the cheap token that does not complete the task costs more than the expensive token that does.

The honest note

  • The note's date ranges, deflation method and chart scaling were not confirmed in the secondary check: the check confirmed the quotes and the note's existence, not every methodological detail.
  • This article does not claim your company gains nothing from AI: it reports what Apollo's note shows at index level and what that suggests for measuring your own project.
  • Sources: Apollo Global Management note (October 24, 2026) via Bloomberg, verified by TrueSeeker against the original document.

Frequently asked questions

Does Apollo say AI is useless?

No: it says that, at index level, outside the Mag 7, revenue per worker and margins are flat with no signs yet of a productivity lift. It is an aggregate reading, not a per-company verdict.

What is the S&P 493?

The S&P 500 minus the seven Mag 7 companies: Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia and Tesla.

Why does it matter for a small business?

Because it separates two questions: how much you spend on AI and how much your process improves. The note suggests that, in aggregate, spending has not yet translated into productivity outside the giants.

Should I stop investing in AI because of this note?

Not on the note alone: measure your own use case with a baseline, an owner and a review date, and decide on your numbers.
