How an AI Agent Works Inside (Without the Jargon)

By inicIA · September 26, 2026 · 8 min read

Direct answer

An AI agent works in a continuous four-step cycle: perceive, reason, act, and remember. It perceives information from its environment, reasons with an AI model to decide the best next step, takes real actions with its connected tools, and stores what it learned in its memory so it can decide better next time.

Think of it as a digital employee: it receives a goal, works on its own, and checks with you only when something needs your judgment. If you want the definition from the business side first, read what an AI agent is and how a business uses one.

The four components of every AI agent

Inside, every AI agent —from the simplest to the most advanced— uses the same four components, working as a team:

ComponentWhat it doesIts equivalent in your company
PerceptionReceives and organizes information: messages, data, documents, events from your systemsThe eyes and ears of an employee
Reasoning and planningAnalyzes the situation and decides the best next step toward the goalThe judgment of a good manager
Tools and actionsTakes real actions: books, updates the CRM, sends messages, checks inventoryThe hands that do the work
MemoryStores the context of the task and learns from what worked to decide betterThe team's notebook and experience

The cycle repeats without stopping: perceive, reason, act, remember. Each round brings it closer to the goal.

Perception: how the agent "sees" your business

Perception is the agent's front door: through it, it receives WhatsApp messages, emails, transcribed calls, CRM records, and events from your systems. Its job is to turn that noise into clear information: when a customer writes "are the Brickell apartments still available?", it identifies the intent, the topic, and the context (that customer asked about Downtown last week). The key rule: with old or incomplete information, the agent decides badly, so it needs live access to your systems.

Reasoning and planning: the brain that decides

At the agent's core sits a language model that works as its brain. It takes the goal ("qualify this lead and book a call"), adds what perception organized and what memory remembers, and builds a plan in concrete steps. The big difference: if a step returns something unexpected, the agent changes the plan. If Saturday's calendar is full, it proposes Sunday without anyone telling it to. That ability to replan is what separates it from traditional automation, which follows a fixed path no matter what.

Tools and actions: the hands that execute

Tools are the agent's hands: the connections to your calendar, your CRM, WhatsApp, your email, or your inventory database. Each tool is one concrete action: check availability, create a record, send a message, update a status. The more well-connected tools it has, the more real work it completes on its own. And this is the test that separates a real agent from a system that only chats: it can change something in your systems —an appointment, a record, an order. To dig deeper into that difference, see chatbot vs virtual assistant vs AI agent: real differences.

Memory: the experience that accumulates

Short-term memory holds the context of the current task: what the customer said two messages ago, which step of the process it's on. Long-term memory persists across conversations: each customer's preferences, what worked in similar cases, your catalog and your business rules. That memory turns a generic agent into your agent: every week it decides with more of your business's judgment, like an employee who knows customers better with each passing month.

The business analogy: it works like your best employee

Together, the four components work like your best employee:

  • It receives a clear goal, like the role you give a new hire: "answer WhatsApp messages and book visits."
  • It perceives what's happening: it reads messages and checks the CRM, like an employee checking their inbox when they arrive.
  • It plans: it decides the order of steps based on each situation, like a good salesperson adapting their conversation to each customer.
  • It acts: it books, updates records, and sends messages. It does the work, on top of reporting it.
  • It learns: it stores what worked and applies it next time, like the employee who comes to know every customer over time.

Its edge over any human employee: it works 24/7, never forgets a follow-up, and scales without you hiring. And you set its limits: what it decides on its own and what needs your approval.

How an agent decides, step by step

Here is the full cycle with a real example: a lead writes on WhatsApp, "I want information about the pre-construction apartments."

  1. Perceives. It reads the message, detects the intent (interest in pre-construction), and checks whether that number has talked to the business before.
  2. Reasons. Its goal is to qualify and book. It plans to check inventory, review the CRM, and reply with the two or three options that fit best.
  3. Acts. It queries the inventory database, updates the CRM record, and sends the message with the options.
  4. Perceives again. The lead replies: "I like the second option, can I visit it on Saturday?"
  5. Reasons and acts again. It checks the calendar, proposes two Sunday time slots, books the chosen one, and sends the confirmation.
  6. Remembers. It stores the preference —pre-construction, weekends— so the next conversation starts with an advantage.

Each round of the cycle takes seconds. In a week, the same agent repeats this cycle across hundreds of conversations without losing track of any of them. For how this kind of work connects with the rest of your operation, read what AI automation is.

What makes an agent work well (or fail)

Here is how you evaluate an agent —yours or a vendor's—:

  • A clear goal. The agent needs to know what success means: a booked appointment, a qualified lead, a resolved case. Without a measurable goal, even the best agent improvises.
  • Up-to-date data. Live connection to your systems matters as much as the model it uses.
  • Well-connected tools. Every action it can execute multiplies its value: with calendar and CRM access it does far more than if it could only write messages.
  • Memory trained on your business. Your rules, your catalog, and your approved responses turn it into your agent instead of a generic one.
  • Defined limits. What it decides on its own and what it escalates to a human. Clear limits are what allow you to give it autonomy with confidence.

Frequently asked questions

Is an AI agent the same as ChatGPT?

ChatGPT on its own is a general-purpose assistant that answers when you ask it something. An AI agent is a system built around a model like that, with connected tools, memory of your business, and permission to act inside your systems. The model is the brain; the agent is the brain plus the hands, the eyes, and the experience.

Can an AI agent make mistakes?

Yes, like any employee. That is why it is configured with clear limits: what it decides on its own, what needs your approval, and how it logs every action. With up-to-date data and well-defined rules, errors drop sharply, and every error gets logged so it can be fixed once.

Do I need technical skills to have an AI agent in my business?

No. You define the goal, the rules, and the limits in business language; the team that implements it handles the technical side. Your role is the same as with a new employee: tell it what to achieve and how success is measured.

How long does it take an AI agent to learn how my business works?

The foundation —your catalog, your rules, your approved responses— is loaded from day one. What improves over time is long-term memory: with every conversation it accumulates context and sharpens its decisions. Within weeks it operates with your business's judgment.
